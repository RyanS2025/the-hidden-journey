The Crumb Trail of a Girl Scout Cookie

Girl Scout cookies come with a much longer backstory than the label lets on. The palm oil was grown somewhere. The cocoa traveled across an ocean. The sugar, the flour, the box, even the truck that delivered them — every piece has a backstory. This site is about tracing those hidden journeys, so the next time someone at a booth asks "are these really sustainable?" you'll actually know.

Why It Matters

Knowing what's in the box helps Scouts answer the questions customers actually ask — about people, places, and the long way ingredients travel.

9,400 mi

Environmental Impact

The palm oil in your Thin Mints crossed 9,400 miles by cargo ship from Indonesia before it became a cookie. Every box carries a footprint, even when you can't see it.

Age 5

Labor & Human Rights

A Malaysian government report found kids as young as 5 working on palm oil plantations alongside their parents. Behind every ingredient is a real person.

6

Global Cooperation

Palm oil from Malaysia, sugar from Brazil, cocoa from the Ivory Coast and Ghana — a single Thin Mint is the result of dozens of countries working together. That's kind of amazing.

You

Consumer Power

Knowing what you're selling is a superpower. When a customer at a booth asks "are these sustainable?", a Scout who has done the homework can answer with real confidence — and that builds trust.

The Emotional Centerpiece

Olivia Chaffin's Story

Photo of Olivia

From news coverage
(with permission)

— Tennessee, age 11 —

"

Olivia Chaffin was one of the best cookie sellers in her Girl Scout troop in Jonesborough, Tennessee. She sold over 600 boxes and earned a badge for it. For her, Girl Scouts was about the idea that girls could actually do good in the world.

When Olivia heard that palm oil production was linked to rainforest loss in places like Indonesia and Malaysia, she started reading the ingredients on her own cookie boxes. Sure enough, palm oil was right there on the label.

Olivia noticed something else, too: a little green tree logo and the words "certified sustainable." At first that was reassuring — it sounded like the palm oil in her cookies wasn't the kind that hurt rainforests or orangutans.

Then she looked closer and saw the word "mixed" on the label. When she looked it up, she learned something surprising. "Mixed" meant that sustainable palm oil had been blended with palm oil from other sources somewhere in the supply chain, so you couldn't trace any specific box back to any specific farm.

So Olivia started asking questions. She asked her troop, she researched certifications, she read about workers and farms across the world. The more she learned, the more she realized how many people, places, and choices go into a single cookie.

Olivia is about 20 now. She stopped selling cookies at 14, and the questions she had been asking eventually turned into a petition that was covered by national news. It started a real conversation about transparency in food supply chains — one that is still pushing the industry to be more open about where ingredients come from.

Every Scout at a cookie booth deserves to be able to answer
the simple question a customer might ask: where does this come from?
